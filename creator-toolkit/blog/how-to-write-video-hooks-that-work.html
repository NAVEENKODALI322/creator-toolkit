How to Write a Hook That Actually Keeps People Watching

Jul 27, 2026 · 5 min read

You can have the best content in your video, and it won't matter if nobody makes it past the first five seconds. That's the entire job of a hook: buy you enough time for the rest of the video to do its job.

Why the First Few Seconds Decide Everything

On both YouTube and Shorts/Reels, viewers make a stay-or-leave decision almost instantly. This isn't viewers being impatient for no reason — it's a reasonable response to a feed full of options. Your hook is competing with everything else on their screen, not just other videos in your niche.

This is why "just start talking" rarely works. A hook needs intention behind it.

Four Hook Types That Work Reliably

1. The Direct Promise State exactly what the viewer will get, clearly and specifically. "I'll show you how to edit this in under 10 minutes" works better than a vague intro, because it sets an expectation the viewer can hold you to — which keeps them watching to see if you deliver.

2. The Curiosity Gap Open with a statement that raises a question the viewer wants answered. "This one setting was destroying my video quality and I didn't notice for six months" makes someone want to know which setting, and why it took so long to catch.

3. The Bold Claim A specific, slightly contrarian statement grabs attention because it interrupts expectation. "Most people are editing wrong, and it's costing them views" works if you can actually back it up in the next few seconds — an unsupported bold claim damages trust fast.

4. The Cold Open Start in the middle of the action — the result, the mistake, the reaction — before rewinding to explain how you got there. This works especially well for tutorial and story-style content, because it shows the payoff before asking for the viewer's time.

What Doesn't Work

  • Slow intros. "Hey guys, welcome back to my channel, today we're going to talk about..." — this is dead time on a platform where the first impression happens in seconds.
  • Hooks disconnected from the content. A hook that promises something the video doesn't deliver increases early drop-off once viewers feel misled, and it damages trust for future videos too.
  • Hooks that are too clever to understand quickly. If a viewer needs three seconds to parse what you mean, you've already lost the two seconds you had.

Testing Your Own Hooks

A simple test: read your hook out loud, alone, with no context. If it doesn't make you want to know what happens next, it won't work on a stranger scrolling past dozens of other videos.

Writing five different hook options for the same video and picking the strongest is a habit worth building — the first version you think of is rarely the best one.
